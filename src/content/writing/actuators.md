A CPU is oddly cold. I remember being surprised by the feeling when I first built my PC. The moment was the culmination of months of research and planning, and dropping the CPU into place was the first step. When I finally watched it power on, I felt an immense sense of pride. I had brought something to life.

This summer, I worked on the acquisition of a physical AI chip designer that let small devices run AI on their own. Kind of fittingly, my first deal was about bringing machines to life. But chips only give a machine a brain. To actually move, it needs muscles, and in robots and drones those muscles are actuators, the motors inside every joint and rotor.

Right now, almost all of those muscles are made abroad, and this year Washington decided that’s a problem. In July, the FCC barred new foreign-produced humanoids, quadrupeds and mobile robots from US authorization, seven months after doing the same to drones and their motors. To count as American-made, a robot now needs 65% of its component cost to be domestic, rising to 75% in 2029, and allied parts don’t count. Actuators are roughly half of a humanoid’s cost, so if they’re foreign, the robot tops out at about 50% domestic, well short of the 65% bar. Without American actuators, there is no American robot. Today that supply barely exists. China builds over 30 million more drone motors and robot actuators a year than the US, and even Tesla, despite its push to build in-house, still sources Optimus actuator components from China.

Motors are already near their efficiency limit, so the winner won’t be whoever builds a marginally better one. It’ll be whoever reaches scale first, by pooling demand from dozens of drone and robot makers that are each too small to justify their own motor line, and by getting designed into their products during the redesign wave these policy changes force.

That’s the plan at Westmag, a San Francisco-based startup that came out of stealth this June with a seed round led by a16z. Westmag is one of the earliest movers looking to fill this gap and I believe it can win for three reasons. First, it’s starting where there is already volume today. The Pentagon is planning to buy more than 200,000 small drones by 2027, and Westmag says it already has committed orders for hundreds of thousands of motors and actuators. Because most robot actuators have a drone-style motor at their core, it builds both on one line. Effectively, drones fund the factory, and robots are the bigger prize. Second, it is moving upstream where no one else will, stamping its own stator steel and finishing its own magnets. That lets it add new sizes quickly and control its costs. Third, the team. CEO David Hansen co-founded the e-bike startup Weel in 2018, traveled to China that year to source its motors, and spent 2020–21 learning the Chinese motor supply chain. COO Jordan Sanders spent 2.5 years as COO at Slip Robotics, scaling the company from launch to hundreds of robots at 25+ sites. 

The biggest risk sits on the drone side, where most of Westmag’s volume is today. Drone motors come in standard sizes and are easy to swap, so the moat there is thin. Unusual Machines already makes about 30,000 a month, and fast-growing drone makers like Neros build their own. On a commodity part, the lowest cost wins, so this is where stamping its own steel and finishing its own magnets has to pay off. Robot actuators are stickier, since they’re custom-fit to each robot, but that market is still a few years out. Westmag has to win enough drone business to reach scale and cost parity before policy protection fades, which means building factories ahead of demand. Before investing, I’d want to understand the capex needed per unit of capacity and how Westmag plans to fund it. The Pentagon has already committed more than $1.4 billion in conditional loans to US drone-component and magnet makers, and debt like that, backed by firm customer orders, would be way cheaper than raising equity at every step.
